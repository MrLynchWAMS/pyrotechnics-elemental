How the Periodic Table is Organized

The Periodic Table arranges all 118 known elements in order of increasing atomic number (the number of protons in an atom's nucleus).

  • Periods (Rows 1–7): Horizontal rows show how many electron shells an atom has.
  • Groups / Families (Columns 1–18): Vertical columns group elements with the same number of valence (outer) electrons, giving them similar chemical properties.

Key Pyrotechnic Families

  • Alkali Metals (Group 1 — Li, Na, K): Soft, highly reactive metals that easily lose one outer electron and produce vivid flame colors (red, yellow/gold, violet).
  • Alkaline Earth Metals (Group 2 — Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba): Reactive metals that burn brightly and give fireworks intense white flashes, orange, red, and green colors.
  • Transition Metals (Groups 3–12 — Ti, Fe, Cu, Zn): Dense metals with high melting points used for bright sparks, blue flame colors, and smoke effects.
  • Post-Transition Metals & Metalloids (Al, Sb): Aluminum burns fiercely for silver-white sparks; antimony creates shimmering glitter trails.
  • Nonmetals & Halogens (C, N, O, P, S, Cl): Provide the fuels, oxygen-rich oxidizers, and chlorine donors that make colors deeper and brighter.

Why Do Elements Make Different Colors?

Inside a burning firework shell, intense heat excites the electrons inside metal atoms, boosting them to higher energy levels.

When those electrons immediately drop back down to their ground state, they release that extra energy as a photon of light. Because every element has a unique electron arrangement, each element emits a signature wavelength (color)—the exact same principle used in a chemistry flame test!
